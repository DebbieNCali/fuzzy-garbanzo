**Theme:** If you dedicate yourself, you can learn and thrive in the AI era — and help others thrive too.

## Post 1: "Learning Math Again (30 Years After I Thought I Was Done)"

### The Hook
Opening scene: Your son hands you a handwritten page titled "Curriculum for Curiosity" - Pre-calculus, trigonometry, calculus, multivariable calculus, linear algebra. You're 30 years out of college. Your last math class was probably in the early 1990s.

### The Counter-Intuitive Insight
**Everyone thinks:** You need to be "good at math" to learn AI
**What's actually true:** You need to be willing to feel stupid again - and that gets easier with age, not harder

### Structure

**Opening (The January 2023 Crisis)**
- January 16: ChatGPT demo, jaw-dropping reactions from Maya, Des, colleagues
- January 20: Google layoffs, whispers about "generative AI"
- The question that haunted you: "Could ChatGPT do my job?"
- Google published "Attention is All You Need" - despair but also hope
- Your son's solution: understand the math behind it

**The Reality Check (What You Were Up Against)**
- 30 years since your last math class
- Full-time job at Google
- Starting from pre-calculus (not even calculus!)
- The specific moment you opened the first textbook/Khan Academy and realized how much you'd forgotten
- The ego hit: feeling like you should know this, but you don't

**What Made It Harder Than Expected**
- It's not just that you forgot the formulas - you forgot *how to learn math*
- The frustration of needing to look up basic concepts you once knew
- Balancing work/life/study (be specific about your schedule)
- The voice that said "you're too old for this" or "real AI people learned this at MIT"

**What Made It Easier Than Expected**
- Age gave you something young learners don't have: patience with not understanding
- You knew *why* you were learning (motivation is different at 50 than at 20)
- You could connect concepts to real things (not just abstract exercises)
- You had resources Khan Academy/YouTube didn't exist when you were in college
- Specific example: 3Blue1Brown video that made something click

**The Specific Path (Summer 2023)**
- How you structured your study time
- Pre-calculus: what you had to relearn
- Trigonometry: the specific concepts that mattered for AI
- Calculus: derivatives and what they actually mean
- Multivariable calculus: gradients, partial derivatives
- Linear algebra: the crown jewel - matrices, vectors, transformations
- ONE specific breakthrough moment per topic (don't list everything, pick the most memorable)

**The First Victory**
- The moment you could actually read a section of "Attention is All You Need" and understand the math
- Not all of it. Just *some* of it. And that was enough.
- How that felt after months of grinding

**The Lesson (Tying Back to Theme)**
- Learning math at 50 isn't harder than learning it at 20 - it's *different*
- You bring life experience, patience, and purpose that young learners don't have
- Dedication doesn't mean being naturally talented - it means showing up when you feel stupid
- The math wasn't the end goal - it was the foundation for understanding what was happening to your industry, your company, your career

**Closing**
- By fall 2023, you were ready to start the UT Austin AI/ML course
- You still didn't feel "ready" - but you were ready enough
- Sometimes dedication means starting before you feel prepared

### Key Takeaways for Readers
1. **The 30-year gap isn't a dealbreaker** - frame it as an advantage (patience, motivation, resources)
2. **You don't need to master everything** - just enough to understand the concepts that matter
3. **Feeling stupid is part of the process** - but it gets easier when you're learning because you *want* to, not because you *have* to
4. **The math is learnable** - concrete path: pre-calc → trig → calc → multivar → linear algebra
5. **Start with why** - your motivation (understanding AI to stay relevant) sustained you through the hard parts

### Specific Details to Include
- The handwritten "Curriculum for Curiosity" page (maybe scan/photo?)
- 1-2 specific videos or resources that helped (3Blue1Brown on linear algebra?)
- The actual timeline: summer 2023, X hours per week, Y months to complete
- One math concept that felt impossible at first, then clicked (with before/after understanding)
- What you were doing at Google during this time (context for "full-time job")

### What Makes This Post Work
- **Vulnerable:** Admits forgetting basics, feeling old, questioning yourself
- **Specific:** Not "I learned math" but "here's exactly what I learned and how"
- **Inspiring:** Shows it's possible without being preachy
- **Useful:** Readers can follow the same path

## Post 2: "The Universe Kept Sending Me Signs (Or: How Interconnections Fueled My AI Journey)"

### The Hook
You're sitting in the back of an MIT classroom, watching your son - whose birth was announced at a Google TGIF 20 years ago - take notes while a former Googler lectures about "Attention is All You Need," the paper that started this whole journey.

### The Counter-Intuitive Insight
**Everyone thinks:** You succeed through isolated individual effort
**What's actually true:** Noticing and following the interconnections gives you momentum you can't generate alone

### Structure

**Opening (The MIT Classroom Moment - Fall 2023)**
- Set the scene in detail: MIT campus, visiting Laker during sophomore year
- Sara Beery teaching AI, former Googler
- "Attention is All You Need" appears on the screen
- Your son in the third row, taking notes
- You just submitted your first UT Austin graded quizzes the night before
- The feeling: hashgacha pratis, God breeze, tailwind

**Tracing the Web of Connections**
Walk through the interconnections chronologically, showing how each built on the last:

1. **Google creates transformer technology** ("Attention is All You Need" published)
2. **Your son is born, announced at Google TGIF** (when Google was small enough for that to be intimate)
3. **You build your career at Google** (20+ years)
4. **January 2023: ChatGPT moment** (technology your company created, now deployed elsewhere)
5. **Your son creates "Curriculum for Curiosity"** (he charts your path to understanding)
6. **Summer 2023: You learn math** (pre-calc through linear algebra)
7. **Fall 2023: You start UT Austin course** (first quizzes, feeling overwhelmed)
8. **The MIT moment: All the threads converge** (Google paper, your son, former Googler, the classroom)

**What the MIT Moment Changed**
- You'd been questioning whether you could do this
- Those first graded quizzes were intimidating
- Imposter syndrome: too old, too late, too far behind traditional CS students
- The MIT classroom moment: validation that you're on the right path
- Not because it proved you were smart - because it showed the connections weren't random
- It gave you momentum to keep going

**The UT Austin Course (Fall 2023 - Spring 2024)**
- PG Program in AI & ML: Business Applications (UT Austin McCombs + Great Learning)
- Why you chose this program (business applications, not pure CS)
- The structure: video lectures, weekend mentorship, hands-on projects
- Balancing full-time work at Google with coursework
- Specific challenges: first projects, learning Python for AI, statistical learning
- Specific breakthroughs: when concepts from summer math clicked in course context
- The 5 hands-on projects + 1 capstone (briefly describe 1-2 that were most meaningful)

**Becoming Valedictorian (The Surprise)**
- You didn't expect this
- What made the difference:
  - **Full-time job as constraint → advantage:** Couldn't context-switch, had to go deep
  - **Business applications focus:** Your Google work experience was more relevant than you thought
  - **Math foundation:** The summer 2023 grind paid off
  - **Asking "dumb" questions:** Coming from outside CS meant you didn't have CS student blind spots
  - **Weekend mentorship sessions:** You used them differently than traditional students
  - **Applying to real work immediately:** Every concept went into your Google work

**The Full Circle Moment**
- Graduating as valedictorian from a UT Austin/McCombs program
- Your son at MIT learning from former Googler
- You at Google applying what you learned
- The transformer technology at the center of it all
- This wasn't just individual achievement - it was being part of an ecosystem

**The Lesson (Tying Back to Theme)**
- Dedication isn't just grinding alone in isolation
- It's noticing when the universe/God/randomness sends you signs
- Following the connections gives you momentum
- Your unique path (Google → son at MIT → UT Austin course → math foundation) couldn't be replicated - and that's the point
- Everyone has their own web of connections - the work is noticing them and following the thread

**Closing**
- Spring 2024: Course complete, valedictorian
- You now understand AI deeply - not just how to use it, but how it works
- The question shifted from "Could ChatGPT do my job?" to "How can I use AI to multiply my impact?"
- That shift unlocked everything that came next

### Key Takeaways for Readers
1. **Notice your unique interconnections** - they're sources of motivation and momentum
2. **Constraints can be advantages** - full-time job forced you to be strategic
3. **Choose programs that match YOUR background** - business applications vs. pure CS
4. **The best path isn't always the most prestigious** - UT Austin/Great Learning online worked better than trying to get into Stanford
5. **Unexpected validation matters** - the MIT moment sustained you through hard weeks

### Specific Details to Include
- Sara Beery's name and her Google background
- Laker's sophomore year timeline
- Your son's birth announcement at Google TGIF (early 2000s Google culture)
- Specific UT Austin course projects (1-2 examples, not all of them)
- What "hashgacha pratis" means (define for readers who don't know Hebrew)
- The actual valedictorian moment/announcement

### What Makes This Post Work
- **Emotionally resonant:** The interconnections are almost too perfect to be fiction
- **Specific:** MIT classroom, Sara Beery, UT Austin projects, not vague "I took a course"
- **Surprising:** The web of connections is genuinely remarkable
- **Humble:** Frames valedictorian as unexpected, attributes it to circumstances + effort
- **Universalizable:** Readers can apply "notice your interconnections" to their own lives

## Post 3: "AI Makeovers: What Happened When I Started Multiplying Others"

### The Hook
A VP at Google comes to you for an "AI Makeover." Two weeks ago, you were helping individual contributors. Last month, you were just trying to save time for yourself. Now you're in a conference room with Stanford researchers observing as you help someone 3 levels above you transform their workflow.

### The Counter-Intuitive Insight
**Everyone thinks:** AI adoption is about tools and training
**What's actually true:** It's about personalized breakthroughs - and the people who can facilitate those breakthroughs become multipliers

### Structure

**Opening (The Shift From Self to Others)**
- Spring/Summer 2024: You finish UT Austin course, you're using AI at work
- Gemini, NotebookLM, other tools - you're "gaining hours of thinking time"
- Specific example: a task that used to take you 20 hours now takes 2
- But you have extra time now - what do you do with it?
- You start helping colleagues informally
- "Can you show me how you did that?"

**The First AI Makeover (Accidentally Inventing a Format)**
- Who was the first person you helped? (name if possible, or role)
- What was their problem/task?
- Walk through what you did:
  - Understanding their specific workflow
  - Identifying where AI could help (not everywhere - specific bottlenecks)
  - Showing them hands-on, not just telling them
  - The breakthrough moment: when they got it
  - Their relief/joy - describe what you saw on their face
- You realized: this is replicable

**Systematizing the AI Makeover**
- What makes an AI Makeover different from "AI training"?
  - **Personalized:** to their specific work, not generic use cases
  - **Hands-on:** you do it together, not lecture-style
  - **Breakthrough-focused:** find the ONE task that will change everything for them
  - **Follow-up:** not one-and-done, you check in
- Your approach:
  - Pre-work: what do they spend time on that they wish they didn't?
  - Session: live problem-solving with AI tools
  - Post-work: they try it on their own, report back
- How long does a typical makeover take?
- What tools do you typically use? (Gemini, NotebookLM, Claude, etc.)

**The Unexpected Scaling**
- You start doing makeovers for more people
- Individual contributors → senior ICs → managers → directors → VPs
- The problems change at each level:
  - ICs: execution tasks (research, analysis, drafting)
  - Managers: synthesis tasks (summarizing team input, strategy docs)
  - VPs: strategic tasks (pattern recognition across org, decision support)
- Specific example at each level (anonymous if needed)

**Stanford Researchers Notice**
- How did they find out about your AI Makeovers?
- They approach you: we want to observe and study this
- Part of a larger AI adoption study at Google
- They observe 30+ makeovers
- Pre- and post-interviews with participants
- What were they measuring? (time saved, satisfaction, behavior change, etc.)
- What did they find? (if you can share preliminary insights)

**The HBR Publication (Q1 2026)**
- Paper accepted to Harvard Business Review
- What does it mean that your informal "helping colleagues" became research?
- The validation: what you invented intuitively has broader applicability
- The responsibility: more people will try this approach

**The Breakthroughs That Keep You Going**
- Share 2-3 specific stories of meaningful breakthroughs:
  - Someone who was drowning in work, now has breathing room
  - Someone who was skeptical of AI, now sees it as a partner
  - Someone who was anxious about being replaced, now feels empowered
- The relief and joy on their faces - that's the fuel
- You have extra time *because* of AI, so you use it to multiply others

**Staying Grounded (The Sabbath Practice)**
- With all this AI work - helping others adopt it, living in it daily - how do you stay human?
- **Going offline on Sabbath**
  - No screens, no AI, no work
  - Reading physical books - unchanging wisdom
  - Connecting to what doesn't change while everything else is changing
- Why this matters:
  - AI is powerful, but it's not wisdom
  - You need to stay inspired to be inspiring
  - The contrast (offline Sabbath → online weekdays) keeps both meaningful
  - You approach AI tools on Monday differently because you disconnected on Saturday

**The Lesson (Tying Back to Theme)**
- You didn't just learn AI to survive - you learned it to thrive *and help others thrive*
- The dedication wasn't the end goal - multiplication was
- Anyone can learn AI for themselves (that's post 1 & 2)
- The real impact is teaching others - not through training, but through personalized breakthroughs
- And staying grounded in unchanging wisdom keeps you human in a rapidly changing world

**Closing**
- From January 2023 ("Could ChatGPT do my job?") to now (multiplying VPs, Stanford research, HBR)
- The arc: fear → learning → mastery → multiplication
- You're the same person who struggled with pre-calculus 18 months ago
- Dedication + noticing interconnections + helping others = thriving in the AI era
- And taking Sabbath rest to stay connected to what matters most

### Key Takeaways for Readers
1. **AI adoption is personal, not generic** - makeovers work better than mass training
2. **Extra time is an opportunity** - use time saved to help others
3. **Breakthroughs scale** - what worked for you can work for others at all levels
4. **Stay grounded** - offset AI immersion with practices that connect you to unchanging wisdom
5. **You don't need credentials to teach** - you just need to have solved the problem yourself first

### Specific Details to Include
- Number of AI Makeovers you've done (30+ observed by Stanford, how many total?)
- Specific tools: Gemini, NotebookLM, Claude 3.5 Sonnet (from your existing blog post), etc.
- Stanford researchers: who they are if you can name them, what department
- HBR paper: timeline (Q1 2026), any details you can share about findings
- Sabbath practice: what you read, how long you've done this, how it feels
- 2-3 anonymized stories of breakthroughs (with permission or sufficiently vague)

### What Makes This Post Work
- **Shows impact beyond self** - you're multiplying others now
- **Credible:** Stanford research + HBR publication validates your approach
- **Humble:** you stumbled into this, didn't set out to be "AI trainer to VPs"
- **Balanced:** Sabbath practice shows you're not AI-obsessed, you stay human
- **Inspiring:** readers can see themselves doing AI makeovers for their teammates
- **Specific:** enough detail that readers could try this approach

## Style Notes (Ben Kuhn Inspiration)

**Do:**
- ✅ Use specific details (names, dates, exact quotes)
- ✅ Be vulnerable about struggles
- ✅ Lead with counter-intuitive insights
- ✅ Show, don't tell (MIT classroom scene, not "it felt meaningful")
- ✅ Give readers actionable takeaways
- ✅ Be honest about what didn't work
- ✅ Use present-tense feeling in key moments

**Don't:**
- ❌ Generic advice ("work hard and you'll succeed")
- ❌ Humble-bragging (frame achievements as surprising, not earned)
- ❌ Listing everything you did (pick the most memorable moments)
- ❌ Preaching (show the journey, let readers draw conclusions)
- ❌ Airbrushing failures (include the doubts, the hard quizzes, the impostor syndrome)
